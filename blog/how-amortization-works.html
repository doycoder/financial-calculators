How Amortization Works: Why Your Mortgage Payment Is Mostly Interest for Years

A mortgage payment looks even, but it isn't. Amortization front-loads the interest, so for the first 18 years most of what you "pay down" isn't your loan at all.

You sign a $300,000 mortgage at 6% and settle in for a steady $1,798.65 a month. It feels equal. It isn't. That first payment quietly funnels $1,500 — over 83% of it — straight into interest, while barely $300 touches your actual loan. Amortization is the engine behind that lopsided split, and not understanding it is one of the most expensive gaps in personal finance.

What Amortization Actually Is

Amortization is simply the schedule that spreads a loan into equal monthly payments over a fixed term, so the debt reaches exactly zero at the end. The payment amount never changes — but its contents do, every single month. In the early years the payment is mostly interest; in the late years it's mostly principal. The word everyone should memorize: front-loaded. The interest is stacked at the beginning of the loan, not spread evenly across it.

Two details jump out. First, the crossover: the month where principal finally outgrows interest doesn't arrive until year 18½. For the first eighteen-plus years, more than half of every payment is interest. Second, the numbers barely move early: after a full decade of payments — about $215,838 — the balance has dropped only from $300,000 to $251,057. Ten years, and you've shaved just $49,000 off the principal.

Why the Interest Is So Front-Loaded

The mechanism is deceptively simple. Each month's interest is charged on the current, still-huge balance — 6% a year on whatever you still owe. At the start you owe the full $300,000, so the interest is enormous and there's little room left in your fixed payment for principal. As the balance slowly shrinks, interest shrinks with it, and the principal portion self-accelerates. Amortization is actually the same compound interest you know, running in reverse — back-loading that now works against the borrower instead of for the saver.

The Brutal Tally: You Pay More Than You Borrow

Add up all 360 payments and the honest total emerges. The $1,798.65 a month comes to $647,515 over the life of the loan — of which $347,515 is pure interest. You borrowed $300,000 and will repay well over $600,000. The interest alone is 1.16× the loan. That's not a fee on the side; it's the majority of what you hand over. How to Beat the Schedule

1. Throw extra at the principal, early

Every extra dollar goes straight to principal, permanently shrinking the balance that future interest is charged on. An extra $100 a month on this $300,000 loan saves about $53,000 in interest and pays the loan off nearly four years early — because the savings compound in reverse.

2. Shorten the term

The same $300,000 at 6% over 15 years costs $2,531.57 a month but only $155,683 in total interest — less than half. A shorter term doesn't just move the payoff date; it deletes the back end of the interest curve where the pain lives.

3. Make extra payments early, not late

An extra dollar thrown at year two does decades of work; an extra dollar at year 28 is nearly pointless. The front-loading cuts both ways — the same asymmetry that makes borrowing expensive makes prepaying powerful, if you do it early.

4. Beware refinancing resets the clock

Refinancing can cut the rate, but it also restarts amortization at month one — meaning you're back to payments that are nearly all interest. Refinance for a lower rate, not just a lower monthly payment.

When Amortization Still Isn't the Whole Story

Four caveats:

  • Fixed vs. variable. Everything above assumes a fixed rate. A variable-rate loan has a moving amortization schedule, so the interest split can shift with the market.
  • Prepayment penalties exist. Some loans charge for paying off early. Check before you funnel every spare dollar into the principal.
  • Opportunity cost. Prepaying a 3% mortgage instead of investing the money at a higher expected return is a real tradeoff. Beating the schedule only wins if the rate you'd earn elsewhere is lower than the rate you'd save.
  • Not every loan amortizes. Interest-only loans and some lines of credit don't reduce principal with each payment. Amortization is a specific design, not a universal law.
