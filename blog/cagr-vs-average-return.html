CAGR vs Average Return: Why Your “10% Average” Was Really 8%

Average return is the arithmetic mean; CAGR is the geometric mean. Volatility drags the real return below the average — and the gap grows with the square of the swings.

A fund advertises a 10% "average annual return" over five years. But if you'd actually held it the whole time, you'd have earned less than 8%. Same fund, same years, two different numbers — both "true." The difference is the silent tax every volatile investment pays: volatility drag.

What Average Return and CAGR Actually Measure

Average return is the arithmetic mean: add up each year's return and divide by the number of years. A +20% year and a 0% year "average" 10%.

CAGR — the compound annual growth rate — is the geometric mean. It's the single constant rate that would take your actual starting balance to your actual ending balance. It's the number you actually lived through.

The Formula

One uses addition, the other uses multiplication:

Average return = (r₁ + r₂ + … + rₙ) ÷ n

CAGR = (Ending ÷ Starting)1/n − 1

They're equal only when every year's return is identical — zero volatility. The moment returns bounce around, the two part ways. It's the same compound interest you already know, applied in reverse to solve for the rate.

The Gap Is Volatility

The cleanest proof is a famous two-year trap. Your investment gains 50% in year one, then loses 50% in year two. The average return is (50 − 50) ÷ 2 = 0%. Zero. Break even — right?

Wrong. $100 becomes $150, then drops to $75. You lost 25% on an investment with a 0% "average" return. The true CAGR is (75 ÷ 100)1/2 − 1 = −13.4% per year. The average said nothing happened; the truth is your money shrank by a quarter.

A Worked Example

Here's a more realistic five-year run — +20%, −10%, +25%, −15%, +30%. A fund would report the average, 10% per year. Here's what actually happened to $100:

0—$100.00

2−10%$108.00
3+25%$135.00
4−15%$114.75
5+30%$149.18

After five years you have $149.18 — a total gain of 49.2%. The average return is 10%; the CAGR is only 8.3%. That 1.7-point gap is the volatility drag, the price of the ride.

Why Funds Quote the Flattering One

A fund or app that leads with "average annual return" is quoting the arithmetic mean — always equal to or higher than the CAGR. It isn't a lie; it's just the friendlier of two truthful numbers. The annualized figure you're shown is usually the one that makes the product look best.

The rule: when in doubt, demand the CAGR. It answers the only question that matters — "what would I have actually earned per year, all in?"

The Rule of Thumb: Volatility Drag

There's a tidy approximation behind the gap: CAGR ≈ average return − (volatility² ÷ 2). The drag scales with the square of the swings — double the volatility and you quadruple the drag.

In the extreme it's brutal. Two funds both average 10% a year; one is smooth and returns exactly 10% each year. The other bounces +50%, −30%, +60%, −40%, +10%. Same 10% average — but the bumpy one's CAGR is just 2.1%. Volatility didn't just shave the return; it ate most of it.

Five Ways to Use This

1. Sanity-check fund marketing

Whenever you see "average return," convert it to a CAGR before deciding anything. The average is the ceiling; your real result lives below it.

2. Compare on CAGR, not average

Two products can share a 10% average and hide a 2% CAGR and a 10% CAGR. Always line up the geometric number, never the arithmetic one.

3. Understand why losses hurt more

A −50% year needs +100% just to break even; a −30% year needs +43%. This recovery asymmetry is where volatility drag shows up in real portfolios.

4. Annualize a single investment the same way

Scale up or down the same math: annualizing one investment's total return to a per-year rate is CAGR, and it's the bridge to ROI.

5. Plan withdrawals around the sequence, not the average

The 4% retirement rule depends on the actual order of returns, not their average — down years early compound against you far harder than the same average would suggest.

When CAGR Still Isn't the Whole Story

Three caveats before you start auditing every fund sheet:

  • CAGR assumes one lump sum with no money added or withdrawn. If you contributed monthly, the right metric is IRR — a dollar-weighted return — not plain CAGR.
  • CAGR flattens the journey. An 8% CAGR that included a brutal 40% drawdown is a very different experience from a steady 8%. Always look at the worst year too.
  • CAGR is backward-looking. It tells you what happened, not what will. A hot five-year CAGR is a rear-view mirror, not a guarantee.
