The 4% Rule: How Much You Need to Retire (and the Risk That Breaks It)

Retire with 25× your annual spending. That's the 4% rule in one multiplication — but the thing that actually breaks retirements isn't the average return, it's the order the returns arrive in.

How much do you need to retire? Strip away the calculators and you land on a shockingly simple number: 25 times your annual spending. The famous 4% rule says you can withdraw 4% of your portfolio in year one and ride it for 30 years. But the rule hides a risk most people never see: what decides whether you go broke isn't your average return — it's the order the returns arrive in.

What the 4% Rule Says

Withdraw 4% of your portfolio in year one, then increase that dollar amount every year with inflation. So $1,000,000 pays you $40,000 in year one, roughly $41,200 the next year (at 3% inflation), and so on for three decades. The rule comes from William Bengen's 1994 research and the later Trinity Study, both of which backtested withdrawal rates against US stock-and-bond history.

The 25× Shortcut

4% is the reciprocal of 25, so the whole rule collapses to one multiplication:

Nest egg = annual spending × 25

Spend $40,000 a year? You need about $1,000,000. Spend $60,000? $1,500,000. Spend $100,000? $2,500,000. The number feels enormous because it is — but that's the honest size of a 30-year cushion.

Want a longer or safer cushion? The math scales cleanly: a 3.5% withdrawal rate means 28.6× your spending, and 3% means 33×. Retiring at 45 instead of 65 forces the lower rate, because the money has to last longer.

Where 4% Came From

Crucial context: 4% isn't the average result — it's the worst-case-to-survive result. Bengen tested 30-year windows across US history and picked the withdrawal rate that survived even the ugliest one: retiring in 1966, just before the 1970s inflation spike and a long, flat market. Recognize what that means — 4% is a floor derived from the harshest stretch of American market history, not a target calibrated to a friendly one.

The Risk No One Talks About: Sequence

Now the part that should reframe how you think about retirement. Two retirees each have $1,000,000, each withdraw $40,000 a year, and each experience the exact same average return of 6.6%. The only difference is when the bad years land.

  • Retiree A retires into a crash — five losing years of −15% come first, then twenty good years of +12%. Same 6.6% average. A runs out of money in year 22.
  • Retiree B retires into a boom — the twenty +12% years come first, then the five −15% years. B ends the 25 years with $2,721,760.

Identical average return, identical withdrawals. One ends broke, the other is a multi-millionaire. That gap is sequence-of-returns risk — and it's why "average return" is a nearly useless number for anyone actually withdrawing money.

Why the First Five Years Decide Everything

The mechanism is what makes it so dangerous: when you're withdrawing, an early loss isn't just a paper loss. You sell shares at the bottom to fund living expenses, locking in the damage permanently, and the portfolio never recovers to its old size. A flat or down first five years is the single biggest hazard a retiree faces. This is the logic behind "bond tents" and target-date funds that turn conservative right around the retirement date.

How to Actually Use the Rule

1. Compute your number

Annual spending × 25. One multiplication, no spreadsheet required.

2. Split "must" from "nice" spending

Cutting your ongoing spending by $10,000 a year shrinks the nest egg you need by $250,000. Spending is the lever most people skip.

3. Stress-test the first five years

The danger zone is years one through five, not year twenty. Plan around the possibility of retiring into a down market.

4. Treat 4% as a target, not a promise

Build in a buffer, or plan to trim withdrawals in a bad first year. Flexibility is cheap; running out is not.

5. Keep the growth engine running

The rule assumes a sensible stock-and-bond mix, not cash. A portfolio left in low-yield cash quietly changes the math.

When 4% Breaks Down

Four caveats:

  • It's a backtest of US history. Japan and several other markets would have supported far less. The future is not obligated to repeat 1966.
  • 4% was built for ~30 years. Earlier retirement calls for 3.5% or 3%, which means a 29×–33× nest egg instead of 25×.
  • It ignores fees and taxes. A 1% advisory fee is a full quarter of your 4% — it has to come out of your spending or your growth.
  • "Success" is a low bar. The rule survives if you don't hit $0 in 30 years. It does not promise you keep your principal or leave an inheritance.
